1. Introduction 
 
Keeping the COE System Relevant 
 
1.1 
Since 1990, the Certificate of Entitlement (COE) system has kept 
Singapore’s vehicle population under control and managed the congestion of our 
roads. Land in Singapore is limited. Roads occupy about 12% of our land area, 
almost as much as housing. With already 660,000 passenger cars shared across 
1.5 million households in Singapore, adding more cars or roads would require 
significantly more land that would compromise other competing demands, such 
as nature and greenery, housing, schools, hospitals, and community facilities.  
 
1.2 
Not everyone in Singapore can drive or wishes to own a car. The 
Government’s priority is to invest heavily in public infrastructure to provide 
accessible, affordable and reliable public transport for all, complemented by 
private transport on a pay-per-use basis, through car sharing, taxis and private 
hire cars.  The revenue from COEs, which averages $5–6 billion annually over a 
ten-year cycle, contributes to the Government’s expenditure on public transport, 
including investments of around $5 billion per year in building new public 
transport infrastructure and renewing existing infrastructure and assets, as well as 
more than $2 billion per year in public transport operating subsidies. 
  
1.3 
The COE system allocates limited vehicle ownership through a market-
based and transparent system. There are currently five COE categories based on 
vehicle type: Category A for “mass-market” cars, Category B for “higher-value” 
cars, Category C for commercial vehicles, Category D for motorcycles, and 
Category E, an open category that can be used to register any vehicle except 
motorcycles. Potential car buyers bid for a COE for their respective vehicles, 
through an open and transparent bidding exercise twice a month; i.e. all bidders 
can see the current clearing price. The final COE clearing price is set at the highest 
unsuccessful bid plus $1; i.e. everyone pays close to what the lowest winning 
bidder bids. Each COE is valid for 10 years.  
 
1.4 
The COE system has kept vehicle population growth sustainable and 
continues to fulfil its fundamental purpose. Over the past 36 years, the 
Government has regularly made adjustments in response to public feedback and 
technological change while preserving its core principles of being market-based 
and transparent.

How has the COE categorisation for cars evolved? 
 
1.5 
The COE categorisation for cars has been refined over the years. Initially, 
passenger cars were grouped into four categories based on engine capacity1. In 
1999, the four categories were consolidated into the current Category A and 
Category B structure to simplify the system and reduce price volatility. We 
decided to retain two car categories to maintain progressivity in the COE system 
by segmenting mass-market and higher-value cars. 
 
1.6 
As vehicle technology evolved, further refinements were made. In 2014, 
an engine power criterion was introduced alongside engine capacity to better 
differentiate between mass-market and higher-performance vehicles. In 2022, the 
Category A power threshold was raised to accommodate newer mass-market 
electric vehicles.  
 
Why are we reviewing COE car categorisation now? 
 
1.7 
When Category A and Category B were first introduced, engine capacity 
and engine power could effectively distinguish between mass-market and higher-
value cars. Over the years, advances in vehicle technology, particularly the 
introduction of electric vehicles into the market, have made this distinction 
increasingly difficult to maintain.  
 
1.8 
As electric cars are software-driven, manufacturers are increasingly able to 
adjust their vehicle specifications to fit within Category A engine power 
thresholds, even for higher-value cars. This has rendered the existing criteria less 
effective in distinguishing between mass-market and higher-value cars. For 
example, the same car model (e.g. Tesla Model Y) can fall into either Category 
A or B depending on its power rating.  Consequently, buyers of mass-market and 
higher-value vehicles increasingly compete for Category A COEs, narrowing the 
difference between Category A and B COE prices. In fact, from Feb to Jun 2026, 
Category A price exceeded the Category B price on three occasions.

1.9 
It is therefore timely to consider how to improve the categorisation of 
passenger cars to better reflect today’s vehicle market while preserving the 
principles of a market-based and transparent COE system. This review aims to 
address the increasing ineffectiveness of the current distinction between mass-
market and higher-value cars. COE prices will continue to be determined by 
demand and the available COE supply and are therefore not a subject of this 
review. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
What have we heard? 
 
1.10 As part of the review, LTA has engaged more than 200 members of the 
public, including academics and representatives from the automotive industry 
through a series of focus group discussions and engagement sessions in April and 
May 2026.  
 
1.11 Participants generally agreed that engine capacity and engine power have 
become less effective at distinguishing between mass-market and higher-value 
cars. The current distinction between Category A and Category B has hence 
become less meaningful, with Category A prices exceeding Category B prices 
more frequently.  
 
1.12 Participants discussed a range of ideas on how COEs could be categorised. 
They suggested combining the current Category A and Category B categories into 
a single COE category for passenger cars, while introducing another mechanism 
to ensure buyers of higher-value cars pay higher COE premiums than mass-
market cars. Ideas raised on the differentiating criterion between cars included 
the car’s Open Market Value (OMV), selling price and physical dimensions. 
There were also suggestions to remove Category E to further simplify the system 
should Categories A and B be merged. 
 
1.13 The proposals set out in this consultation paper have taken on board these 
views.

2. Proposal – Single Passenger Car Category with a Fee-and-Rebate 
(“Feebate”) System  
 
LTA is seeking views on the following: 
a. Whether Categories A and B should be merged into a single 
passenger car category, with a fee-and-rebate (“feebate”) system 
based on the value of the car, and if so, how it should be designed; 
and  
b. Whether Category E should be adjusted.  
 
Guiding Principles 
 
In reviewing the COE categorisation for cars, LTA is guided by three principles. 
 
2.1 
The COE framework allocates vehicle ownership in the most efficient 
manner, so that those who place the greatest value on owning a vehicle are able 
to do so.  
 
2.2 
The framework should ensure that buyers of higher-value cars pay more 
for a COE than those of lower-value cars. 
 
2.3 
The framework should be open and transparent, so buyers understand how 
the system works. 
 
LTA’s Proposal 
 
2.4 
We propose to merge Categories A and B into a single car category, as the 
distinction between both categories has become less meaningful. In addition, 
from the perspective of road usage and congestion, a mass-market car and a 
higher-value car have broadly the same impact on road space. Merging 
Categories A and B into a single pool would create a larger pool of COEs, which 
could also help reduce volatility in the COE price.  
 
2.5 
However, merging Categories A and B would also mean that buyers of 
mass-market cars would compete directly with buyers of luxury cars in the same 
pool. To ensure that buyers of luxury cars pay a higher premium than buyers of 
mass-market cars, a feebate system can be introduced, where buyers of mass-
market cars would receive a rebate, while buyers of luxury cars would have to 
pay additional fees.

2.6 
A key consideration is how to determine the value of a car for the purposes 
of the feebate. The current criteria for Categories A and B, engine capacity and 
engine power, were designed for a different purpose and may not adequately 
reflect the value or market positioning of cars today.  
 
2.7 
During the engagements, some participants suggested using the 
dimensions of a car as the basis for categorisation. While simple to understand, 
vehicle size does not consistently distinguish between mass-market and higher-
value cars. For example, a luxury sports car may be physically smaller than a 
mass-market family multi-purpose vehicle.  
 
2.8 
One way to differentiate between the market positioning of cars is based 
on their value. Under a feebate system: 
a. Buyers of lower-value cars would receive a rebate; 
b. Buyers of mid-range cars would see no adjustment; and 
c. Buyers of higher-value cars would pay an additional fee. 
 
2.9 
The applicable rebate or surcharge would be applied to the prevailing COE 
price. This will maintain a meaningful difference in the amount paid by buyers of 
mass-market and luxury cars, even though they bid in the same category.  
 
2.10 Our proposals to merge Categories A and B and introduce a feebate system 
are to re-introduce the distinction between mass-market and higher-value cars. 
The objective is not to influence overall COE prices, which will continue to be 
determined by demand and the available COE supply. 
 
2.11 We would like to seek your views on whether a single passenger car 
category with a feebate system based on the value of the car should be 
introduced.

How should the feebate system be designed? 
 
2.12 There are two key questions to be addressed when designing a feebate 
system: 
a. How should the value of a car be determined for the purposes of 
assigning a surcharge or rebate; and 
b. How many feebate bands should there be, and what should the 
rebate/surcharge quantum be for each band.  
 
2.13 Determining the value of the car. The value of a car is influenced by a range 
of factors, especially in a diverse car market. During our engagements, 
participants suggested various ways of differentiating between mass-market and 
higher-value cars, including using the car’s Open Market Value (OMV) and 
selling price. However, selling prices can vary widely, and are often influenced 
by other factors such as marketing, discounts, dealer practices (e.g. overtrade), 
and other non-vehicle related considerations (e.g. whether financing is taken up).  
There are many ways to structure transactions to artificially depress the declared 
selling price. This would require additional safeguards which would add 
complexity and compliance costs.  
 
2.14 In comparison, the OMV is a good starting point. It represents the value at 
which the vehicle was imported, and is currently used to calculate the Additional 
Registration Fee (ARF). Importers are required to submit documents to Customs 
as proof of the value at which the vehicle was imported, and Customs takes strong 
action against false declarations. However, the value at which a vehicle is 
imported can fluctuate over time, depending on factors such as exchange rates 
and differences in sourcing and supply chains.  
 
2.15 We therefore propose using median OMV to determine the COE 
feebate band for each car model. LTA will determine the median OMV of each 
car model (e.g. Toyota Sienta) using past data if available and alternatives (e.g. 
the OMV of the specific vehicle) as proxy if not. The median OMV will then be 
used to determine the applicable feebate band for the car model. The feebate 
bands would be set based on the OMV percentile of cars. LTA will review the 
banding of car models annually, taking into account changes in the car market 
and new models introduced.  
 
2.16 The feebate band assigned to each car model would be published annually 
so that buyers know the applicable COE rebate or surcharge before deciding 
which car to purchase. Car retailers who wish to seek a review of the band 
assigned to a particular car model may appeal to LTA.

2.17 Setting of feebate bands and surcharge/rebate amounts. The feebate should 
maintain a meaningful difference in the overall cost of obtaining a COE for lower 
and higher-value cars, even though all car buyers would bid for COEs from the 
same pool.  In considering the appropriate rebate and surcharge amounts, one 
possible reference point is the historical price gaps between Category A and 
Category B. This provides an indication of the difference in COE premiums that 
buyers of the two groups of cars have historically been willing to pay under the 
existing system. 
 
2.18 There is also a trade-off between simplicity and the degree of 
differentiation when determining the number of bands. While having fewer bands 
would be easier to operationalise and communicate, the difference in feebate 
quantum between bands will be sharper. Having more bands would allow for finer 
differentiation between cars of different values, but would make the system more 
complex. Two options for structuring the feebate bands, and the corresponding 
rebate/surcharge amounts, are summarised below for views:  
 
a. Option A: Three-Band Structure (see Table 1). Under this option, cars 
would be grouped into three broad bands. Cars in Band 1 would receive 
a $15,000 rebate, cars in Band 2 would have no adjustment, and cars in 
Band 3 would pay a $15,000 surcharge. The three-band structure would 
be simpler to administer. However, with fewer bands, the adjustments 
between adjacent bands would be larger. 
 
b. Option B: Five-Band Structure (see Table 2). Under this option, cars 
would be grouped into five bands: cars in Band 1 would receive a 
$15,000 rebate, followed by a $7,500 rebate for Band 2 and no 
adjustment for Band 3. Cars in Bands 4 and 5 would pay surcharges of 
$7,500 and $15,000 respectively. The five-band structure would allow 
for smaller adjustments between adjacent bands. This would reduce the 
difference in treatment between broadly comparable cars that fall into 
adjacent bands. However, having more bands would make the 
administrative process more complex than the three-band structure. 
 
c. Table 1 and Table 2 show the indicative banding of typical models 
based on cars registered in 2025. The banding would be reviewed 
annually if the feebate system is introduced.

Should Category E be adjusted? 
 
2.21 Category E COEs, which can be used to register any Category A, B or C 
vehicle, were intended to provide flexibility in the COE system to accommodate 
changes in demand for different categories of vehicles. Category E also caters to 
the needs of urgent buyers, as Category E Temporary COEs4 are transferable if 
they were bid for by an individual. 
 
2.22 While Category E draws its quota from Categories A, B and C5, bidders 
typically use Category E COEs to register vehicles in the category with the 
highest COE price. In recent years, Category E COEs have been mostly used for 
Category B cars. If Categories A and B are merged into a single category, 
Category E will only be used to register vehicles in two categories (i.e. the merged 
car category and Category C), and will continue to be used predominantly to 
register cars, at the expense of Category C. With the possible merger of 
Categories A and B, we would also like to review the treatment of Category E. 
 
2.23 One option suggested by participants is to remove Category E to further 
simplify the system. However, this would adversely impact buyers who urgently 
need to purchase a car, as they would need to wait for the next COE bidding 
exercise to do so. An alternative could therefore be to retain Category E for 
registration of cars only. This way, car buyers with urgent needs will continue to 
have options, without impacting Category C COE supply. 
 
2.24 We would like to seek your views on how Category E should be 
adjusted.

3. Other Suggestions We Heard 
 
3.1 
In addition to the proposals in this consultation paper, we also heard other 
suggestions raised during our engagements. We explain below why some of these 
suggestions may not be suitable for implementation at this point of time. 
 
(A) Allocating COEs based on family needs 
 
3.2 
Many participants suggested that families with young children, particularly 
larger families, should receive greater support with car ownership. This would 
make it easier to manage school runs, childcare, caregiving responsibilities and 
family travel. These are understandable concerns, and LTA recognises that 
families have different transport needs at different stages of life. The key question 
is how best to support these needs while preserving a COE system that remains 
market-based, equitable, and transparent. 
 
3.3 
Choosing to allocate COEs based on household circumstances requires 
judgement about whose needs should receive greater priority. Allocating more 
COEs for one group such as families with young children would necessarily mean 
fewer COEs for other groups such as caregivers and multi-generational 
households, who may also have compelling transport needs. Furthermore, 
household circumstances also change over time, making it difficult to administer 
such a system fairly over the lifespan of a COE.  
 
3.4 
Instead, the transport needs of large families can be supported in ways 
beyond car ownership. As announced by PM Wong at the National Day Rally 
2026, the Government will provide more help with the transport needs of larger 
families. LTA is working out the details of a transport grant to help offset the 
transport costs of large families with three or more Singapore Citizen children, 
whether by taxi or public transport. LTA will announce more details on this grant 
when ready. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
“I have three young kids. Not because the car is a must, it just makes life so 
much easier for large families with children.”

(B) Supporting Individuals to Bid Directly for COEs 
 
3.5 
Some participants suggested that motor dealers should not be allowed to 
bid for COEs on behalf of their customers, to make the bidding process fairer and 
more transparent for individual buyers. On the other hand, others shared that they 
preferred dealers to continue bidding on their behalf due to convenience as they 
focus primarily on the total cost of the car. While some buyers want greater 
control over their COE bids, others value having dealers provide a convenient 
one-stop service. 
 
3.6 
In a market-based system, rather than restricting who can bid, we can make 
it easier for individuals to participate directly if they choose to do so, while 
preserving the convenience of dealer-assisted bidding for buyers who prefer this. 
We are looking at ways to encourage individuals to bid. 
 
3.7 
Today, individuals can submit their bids via DBS/POSB Automated Teller 
Machines (ATMs) and revise them online. For motor dealers, both the submission 
and revision of bids can be done online. Individuals and motor dealers pay a fee 
of $2 or $8/$10 respectively per bid made or bid revision. We are working on 
allowing individuals to submit their initial bid online as well as reviewing the fees 
payable per bid to encourage individuals to do so. 
 
3.8 
There are also many suggestions to require motor dealers to be more 
transparent, such as by publishing unbundled car prices without the COE. We are 
looking at ways to do so. This would improve price transparency and make it 
easier for buyers to compare prices across dealers and purchase cars directly with 
their own COEs. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
“Let would be car owners bid for their COEs. Car dealer can only sell their 
cars or vehicles without COE. They are not to be involved in the COE 
bidding for car owner.”

(C) Pay-as-you-bid (PAYB) 
 
3.9 
Some participants suggested replacing the current Lowest-Successful-Bid 
system, where the clearing price is set at the highest unsuccessful bid plus $1, and 
all successful bidders pay the same price. Under a PAYB system, each successful 
bidder would instead pay the amount of his or her own bid. Some participants 
believe that this may encourage bidders to submit more conservative bids and 
therefore lower COE prices.  
 
3.10 However, it is unlikely that PAYB would result in lower COE prices. 
Firstly, a PAYB auction does not alter bidders’ underlying willingness to pay or 
the number of COEs available. Bidders with the highest willingness to pay would 
generally continue to secure the available COEs. The main difference is that 
successful bidders would pay their own bid instead of a common clearing price. 
 
3.11 Secondly, as the COE auction is conducted openly today, bidders can 
observe the prevailing price and revise their bids during the exercise. Under such 
a system, even if bidders were to pay what they bid, a rational bidder would 
always bid marginally above the prevailing price. This would be no different from 
the current system. 
 
3.12 Across bidding exercises in 2025, around two in three Category A and B 
bids were placed within 5% above or below the eventual clearing price. Of the 
successful Category A and B COE bids, around three in four were secured at bids 
within 5% of the eventual clearing price (Figure 1). Given that most bids today 
already cluster around the eventual clearing price, PAYB is unlikely to materially 
change bidding behaviour, allocation outcomes or overall COE price levels.

3.16 A separate COE category for PHCs would also mean taking some quota 
away from Categories A and B. If too few COEs are set aside for PHCs, there 
will be fewer PHCs, which could lead to higher rental rates for drivers and higher 
fares for commuters. On the other hand, taking too many COEs away from 
Categories A and B will mean fewer COEs for individual car buyers and lead to 
even higher COE prices.  
 
3.17 PHCs can also be used for both ride-hail services and personal journeys. 
Setting them aside in a separate category would require additional regulatory 
measures to ensure that vehicles are used only for their intended purpose. 
Addressing these issues will require ongoing usage requirements and monitoring, 
adding complexity to the COE framework for all drivers. 
 
(E) Surcharge on Multiple Car Ownership   
 
3.18 Some participants suggested introducing an additional surcharge for 
second and subsequent cars, similar to the Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty 
(ABSD) for residential property. This would discourage multiple-car ownership 
and make more COEs available for first-time or single-car owners. 
 
3.19 Multiple-car ownership has been declining. In 2025, about 66,000, or 
approximately 13%, of car-owning households owned more than one car, down 
from about 87,000, or approximately 19%, in 2012 (Figure 2). While a surcharge 
could moderate demand from households seeking to own multiple cars, and 
distribute the quota across a broader pool of households, multiple-car owners are 
not a key driver of demand in today’s COE market. A surcharge on multiple-car 
ownership is unlikely to have a significant impact on COE prices.

4. Conclusion 
 
4.1 
This consultation paper sets out proposals to keep the COE categorisation 
for cars fit-for-purpose and address the increasing ineffectiveness of the current 
distinction between mass-market and higher-value cars as our vehicle market 
continues to evolve.  
 
4.2 
In summary, LTA is seeking your views on the following: 
a. Whether Categories A and B should be merged into a single passenger 
car category, with a fee-and-rebate (“feebate”) system based on the 
value of the car, and if so, how it should be designed; and 
b. Whether Category E should be adjusted. 
 
4.3 
Your feedback will help us better understand different perspectives and 
inform LTA’s review. This review is expected to be completed by the end of 
2026. Please submit your responses at https://go.gov.sg/coe-pcd-feedback by 2 
November 2026.  
 
4.4 
Thank you for participating in this consultation.
